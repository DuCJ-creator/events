Between the 111 and 114 academic years, prompts shifted from abstract philosophical inquiries toward exploring the intersection of personal identity and societal impact. Fluency alone no longer wins — success requires synthesizing individual agency with a national narrative.

Four dominant categories

  • Abstract Ethics (111) — the fundamental necessity of ethics for a cohesive society.
  • Futuristic Speculation (112) — conceptualizing inventions that foster a more improved and harmonious world.
  • Societal Debates (113) — the duality of technology: a cornucopia of information, or a source of harm.
  • Cultural Reflection (114) — the core beliefs defining Taiwanese identity and its intangible cultural heritages.

The 114 "Cultural Identity" prompt marks the peak of this trend — it asks students to define a polity and civic nature through shared values, not simply describe tradition.

In competition, excellence transcends grammar — it is measured by depth of insight and the use of Current Event Anchors to demonstrate civic awareness.

Thematic Resonance & "Sentimental Taiwan"

High-scoring papers link personal narrative to the national psyche — recognizing everyday treasures, such as the "Heroes of Shovel" who swarmed into Hualien after the recent disaster, called by conscience.

Strategic Structure

Essays move from the Specific (personal anecdote) to the Universal (national or global implication) — the "Taiwan Can Help" slogan is a classic bridge into Taiwan's role as a global citizen.

1. Thematic mapping of the 19 issues

Align the 108 Curriculum's major issues with likely prompts — e.g. "Gender Equity Education" folds into a "Multicultural Education" prompt via the legalization of same-sex marriage as a "live and let live" attitude.

2. The PEEL command

Every body paragraph follows Point → Evidence → Explanation → Link. The Explanation must go beyond restating facts, analyzing the social or philosophical implication of the evidence.

3. Personal archive building

Develop a bank of versatile narratives. One story of community volunteering can be reshaped to fit Character Education, Disaster Prevention Education, or Social Participation.

The Tension Between Growth and Preservation

Introduction — Taiwan, eminently touted for its cornucopia of natural treasures, from Mount Jade to Taroko Gorge, sits at a precarious intersection of progress and preservation. As a nation straddling the Pacific, we prioritized industrial development to join the "Asian Tigers." Yet our true Taiwanese identity is not found in steel and silicon, but in our indomitable bond with the Mother of Nature — one that demands a virtuous cycle of sustainable stewardship.

PLocal resilience is often the most potent defense against environmental degradation.
EDuring recent Hualien landslides, the "Heroes of Shovel" swarmed the mud-caked villages, restoring the land with meticulous dedication.
EThis collective mobilization proves that Disaster Prevention is a communal virtue rooted in a sincere belief in protecting our homeland — not merely a technical skill.
LThis spirit of local activism founds a more resilient, environmentally conscious society.
PNationally, we must shift from exploitation toward an Environmental Education that values intangible heritages.
EThe conflict between semiconductor expansion and water conservation shows the need for policy-driven synergy between technology and nature.
EIntegrating conservation into national identity affirms that growth is unsustainable if it severs the bond between people and land.
LNational policy must reflect the same empathy citizens show their environment during a crisis.
PGlobally, ecological health is a prerequisite for Taiwan's status as a respected global citizen.
E"Taiwan Can Help" should extend beyond medical resources into marine education and biodiversity preservation.
EFailing to steward our landscapes risks losing the very traits that make us "Formosa."
LEnvironmental ethics are a vital component of our international competence, not merely a local concern.

Conclusion — This tension is a test of our collective wisdom and Self-Affirmation. Drawing on solidarity and resilience, economic success need not cost our natural heritage — protecting the Mother of Nature paves a greener future for the next generation.

Strength in Diversity

Introduction — Taiwan's cultural fabric is a tapestry woven from a myriad of beliefs and traditions — Indigenous groups, Hakka people, post-1949 migrants, and recent immigrants — into a functionally independent polity defined by its inclusive nature. Our diversity is our most significant competitive advantage, fostering an open-minded civic nature for a harmonious, globalized world.

PEveryday kindness shows how Multicultural Education is ingrained in Taiwanese hearts.
EWe see it in hospitality toward foreigners on public transport — seat-yielding, unconditioned assistance.
EThis hospitality is an intangible cultural heritage: social participation predicated on empathy that transcends urban self-interest.
LThese micro-interactions form the bedrock of a tolerant, cooperative community.
PBroader traditions — the Mazu pilgrimage, Mid-Autumn Festival — serve as "Sentimental Taiwan" anchors.
EHakka, Hokkien, and immigrant communities gather at these events to share food and togetherness.
ECooperation during ritual proves social harmony comes from synergy of unique characteristics, not cultural erasure.
LShared rituals are the glue maintaining national solidarity amid internal diversity.
PGlobally, Taiwan's commitment to diversity and human rights sets a profound example.
EAs the first Asian nation to legalize same-sex marriage, Taiwan manifested "live and let live."
EThis inclusiveness broadens our global vision, letting us participate in discourse as a progressive democracy.
LInternal diversity is the primary engine of our international relevance and moral authority.

Conclusion — Taiwanese culture embraces a multitude of backgrounds while holding indomitable solidarity. Through everyday empathy and tolerance enshrined in law, we've built a peaceful paradise — and diversity will keep guiding Taiwan as a beam of hope on the world stage.

Digital Responsibility in the Age of Intelligence

Introduction — Entering the AI era, technology presents a groundbreaking duality: a cornucopia of information, and a threat to the fragile bonds of our social fabric. Information Education is no longer a technical requirement but a moral imperative — integrity and Social Participation must be meticulously manifested online, so technology serves a more improved and harmonious world.

PPersonal integrity online is the most indispensable factor in preventing the erosion of trust.
EDuring the Hualien disaster, citizens used the internet to coordinate the "Heroes of Shovel" without seeking personal benefit.
ETechnology "called by our conscience" becomes a beam of hope piercing the darkness of crisis.
LOur digital footprints must reflect real-world commitment to the common good.
PSocietal harmony depends on our collective ability to amplify "Sentimental Taiwan" online.
E"Taiwan Can Help," which trended globally, was a digital manifestation of willingness to assist during COVID-19.
EChoosing warmth over misinformation creates a virtuous cycle reinforcing our compassionate, capable identity.
LDigital responsibility extends Character Education and civic duty.
PGlobally, ethical technology use protects human dignity against narrowly defined success.
EUsed without empathy, AI and the internet risk rendering nations into isolation and chaos.
EAs global citizens, futuristic inventions must respect an inclusive world — "no man is an island."
LThe synergy of human ethics and digital power is the only path to a future lit rather than shadowed.

Conclusion — Technology changes our tools, not the intangible heritage of the heart. Applying ingrained hospitality and resilience to the digital world lets us build a future both technologically advanced and humanely connected.
